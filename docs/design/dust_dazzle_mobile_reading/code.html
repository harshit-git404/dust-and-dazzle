Ch. I • Under the Banyan Tree

Under the Banyan Tree

by Ajeet Kumar Singh

❦

The dust of Sarai never truly settled; it hung suspended in the afternoon glare like pulverized amber, catching the sun until the whole lane felt submerged in golden water. Whenever the midday wind rose from the fallow mustard fields, it brought the tang of dry cow-dung briquettes and the low, collective groan of the grove. At the junction where the earthen cart-track forked toward the river ghāt stood the Great Banyan—older than the oldest grandmothers, older than the British surveyor’s granite marker sunk beside the well.

To a nine-year-old boy barefoot upon the baked mud, the tree was not foliage but an entire continent. Its aerial roots had descended over generations, striking root, thickening into gnarled columns that formed labyrinthine chambers of dappled jade and shadow. It was here that Babuji sat each afternoon upon a worn jute charpai, his spectacles perched halfway down his nose, an ink bottle balanced precariously on the cane frame.

Under those roots, the frantic heat of May seemed suspended by decree. The village elders would gather as the sun peaked, their lungis tucked high, unspooling memory after memory. They spoke of the famine year of ’43, of the canal survey of ’62, and of the bullock carts that once took three full nights to reach the railhead at Gorakhpur. Babuji seldom interrupted; he simply recorded signatures, verified revenue receipts with a fountain pen that scratched like a cicada, and let the banyan keep counsel.

“In Sarai, shade was not merely the absence of sun; it was a physical shelter carved from centuries of slow wood and quiet patience.”

When the evening bells began at the Hanuman shrine by the canal, the shadows would stretch past the mustard storehouses. Babuji would cap his pen with a satisfying brass click, fold his unbleached cotton shawl over one arm, and tap his lathi against the tree trunk twice—a quiet greeting or perhaps an apology for having taken shelter another day. We would walk home along the edge of the canal, our shadows growing impossibly long, leaving the banyan to drink the quiet blue of twilight alone.

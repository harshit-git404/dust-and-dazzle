TALES FROM A VILLAGE AND A CITY

Chapter I: Under the Banyan Tree

by Ajeet Kumar Singh
✦

The noon sun over Sarai was not merely warm; it was an ancient weight that settled into the dry ruts of the bullock road, pressing the entire world into an unhurried, breathless stillness. In those long summers of 1974, before asphalt roads sliced through the mango orchards, the village lived by the cool, sprawling shadow of the Great Banyan. Its aerial roots hung like braided ropes of time itself, anchoring memories that neither the rain nor the monsoon storms could wash away.

Babuji would walk to the edge of the pond just past eleven in the morning, barefoot, his cotton dhoti tucked neatly at the waist. In his breast pocket rested the little nickel pocket watch his father had brought back from Kanpur in 1942. It rarely ran on time, but time in Sarai had no use for mechanical ticks. We measured the passing hour by the angle of the shade slanting across the whitewashed well and the low moaning of the water buffaloes submerged in the duckweed.

"Listen to the roots, Ajeet," he would tell me, tapping his wooden walking stick lightly against the damp, subterranean earth. "A tree that has drunk the groundwater of a hundred monsoons remembers every flood, every famine, and every boy who climbed its branches only to leave for the capital."

“We measure the distance of our departure not in railway miles, but in how softly we remember the creak of the village well at twilight.”

Behind us, the pond shimmered like beaten brass under the zenith. Women from the northern hamlet came down with brass pitchers balanced atop coiled cloths upon their heads, their voices muffled by the rustle of dry neem foliage. A kingfisher darted from the low bamboo culm, piercing the green water like a sapphire needle, then returned empty-beaked to shake the crystal drops from its breast.

Later that afternoon, when the wind finally turned from the east carrying the damp breath of distant rains, Babuji drew his spectacles from his tunic and uncapped his black fountain pen. On the blank margins of an old railway timetable, he began noting the birthweights of the calves and the price of jaggery at the weekly haat. There was no urgency in his strokes, only the steady devotion of a man recording a world that was already quietly slipping into legend.

By nightfall, the fireflies would rise from the wet reeds beneath the banyan, flickering in quiet communion with the oil lamps lit along the village verandas. We sat together in the courtyard until the embers of the dried cow-dung hearth turned to soft lilac ash, listening to the elders recount stories that had neither beginning nor end.

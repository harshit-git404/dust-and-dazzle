Chapter I

Under the Banyan Tree

by Ajeet Kumar Singh

◊ wb_twilight ◊

The noon sun over Sarai did not merely illuminate; it pressed downward like an iron weight upon the red tiled roofs. Everything human, beast, and bird was driven into the sprawling shelter of the Great Banyan at the eastern ridge of the village pond. Its aerial roots, heavy as ship’s ropes, dangled into the cool, undisturbed dust, weaving a green cathedral whose temperature seemed preserved from a forgotten season.

Beside the thickest root trunk sat Babuji’s battered brass water jug, beaded with cold sweat. A wet muslin rag was swaddled around its neck, and every half-hour, when the dry westerly wind—the searing loo—swept the surrounding mustard stubble into mini-cyclones of ochre grit, he would tilt the jug and pour an offering of river-water directly down his throat without letting the vessel touch his lip. The water tasted of sweet wet pottery and deep subterranean roots.

At two in the afternoon, the world went completely still save for the distant passenger train from Muzaffarpur. Its iron whistle arrived muted, dragged across miles of baked mango orchards, a shrill and sorrowful note that made young boys dream of telegraph poles and sea water. We lay on hand-woven charpoys beneath the canopy, our bare skin resting against coarse hemp rope, staring up into the labyrinth of green leaves where green pigeons rested like emerald stones.

Nobody spoke of politics or drought beneath that canopy; speech was conserved like well-water. When old Thakur Ramanand cleared his throat, it was only to recount the flood of 1954, when the Gandak River rose high enough to lap against the banyan’s lower boughs, marooning two wild leopards and a calf in an uneasy, terrified truce until the waters receded. We listened with closed eyes, feeling the immense age of the bark pressed against our shoulders.

“In Sarai, shade was not merely the absence of sun; it was a physical shelter carved from centuries of slow wood and quiet patience.”

— Memoir Notes, p. 44

By four o'clock, the harsh golden glare of the sun had softened into the honeyed hue of mustard oil. The village cattle began their rhythmic trudge home from the dry pastures, their hooves churning the dust into a glowing amber mist that hung suspended in the twilight air. Babuji stood up, shook his white cotton gamchha into the breeze, and whispered his customary evening prayer to the trunk. The tree stood, as it had before our grandfathers drew breath, indifferent to the coming night.

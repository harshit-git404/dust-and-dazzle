The dusk did not arrive quietly in Belahara; it announced itself with the sharp metallic click of Babuji's brass scissors cutting the charred ends of braided cotton wicks. Every evening at quarter past six, the back veranda transformed into a quiet apothecary of glass chimneys and tin funnels. A pungent, intoxicating haze of refined shale mineral oil mingled with the fragrant smoke of burning dried neem leaves, lit by Ma to banish the river mosquitoes.

There was an art to pouring kerosene without spilling a single amber droplet upon the red-clay floor tiles. Babuji would hold his breath, his spectacles slipping gently down his nose, watching the green tin funnel gargle the pungent liquid until the reservoir felt heavy in his palm. If even one splash slipped, the scent would cling to the woven charpoy rugs for three long days, reminding everyone that light in those times always exacted a fragrant, sticky penance from the hands that tended it.

By seven, when the crickets started in the bamboo groves, the three hurricane lamps were carried out—one to the entrance gate to warn passing bullock carts of the seasonal drainage ditch, one to the study table where my open Hindi primers lay dusted with powdered chalk, and the gentlest one to Ma's prayer corner beside the tulsi plant in the inner court. The amber flame shook whenever someone crossed the wooden threshold, fluttering like an imprisoned yellow moth inside its spotless glass chamber.

“Darkness was not an enemy in those village nights; it was merely the velvet fabric upon which the kerosene wick embroidered its quiet golden circle.”

Years later, upon arriving at the roaring electrified terminus of Lucknow junction, the sheer glare of fluorescent tube-lights hurt the eyes with a sterile white vengeance. In that dazzling city of ceaseless current, no one ever smelled the twilight arriving. Light had become thoughtless, stripped of its brass scissors, its measured oil, and its evening prayer.
